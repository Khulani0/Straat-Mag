Every year the South African state buys goods and services on a scale no private customer can match: construction, catering, cleaning, software, security, stationery. For an SMME, government can be the anchor client that changes everything, or the slow payer that kills the business. The difference is mostly knowledge. Here is how the system works, verified against the current rules.

## Step one: the Central Supplier Database

Nothing happens until you are on the Central Supplier Database (CSD). Since 1 April 2016 it has been the single source of supplier information for all spheres of government: national, provincial and municipal. Registration is free at csd.gov.za and you need three things in order first: your CIPC company registration, your SARS tax compliance status pin, and bank details confirmed by your bank. The CSD verifies directors and members against Home Affairs records. Once registered you get an MAAA supplier number, which every bid document will ask for. Keep the record current: banking changes must be updated promptly, and an expired tax status quietly disqualifies you from awards.

## Step two: finding the work

Tenders above the small-value thresholds are advertised on the national eTenders portal, and organs of state also advertise on their own sites and in provincial gazettes. Below tender thresholds, departments buy through written quotations sourced from CSD-registered suppliers, which is where many small businesses actually start: quotation work is smaller, faster and less contested than formal tenders. Being registered, compliant and known to the buyers in your district is a real pipeline.

## The rulebook is changing right now

Public procurement currently runs on a patchwork centred on the Preferential Procurement Policy Framework Act and Treasury regulations. That patchwork is being replaced. The Public Procurement Act 28 of 2024 was signed on 23 July 2024, creating a single framework for all state procurement, but it is not yet fully in force: it commences in phases as its regulations are finalised.

The key document to watch landed this year. On 16 April 2026, National Treasury published the draft General Public Procurement Regulations for public comment, with the comment window closing on 15 June 2026. The draft introduces a tiered preference structure, and the headline for small business is the proposed set-aside: for contracts estimated below R20 million, procuring institutions must reserve bids for identified categories, including black people, women, persons with disabilities, military veterans, youth and small enterprises in a geographic area. If the final regulations keep that shape, sub-R20 million government work becomes structurally friendlier to SMMEs than it has ever been. The Ledger will report the final regulations when they are gazetted.

## The 30-day rule: your rights when the invoice is in

The law on payment is unambiguous. Treasury Regulation 8.2.3, issued under the Public Finance Management Act, requires that all payments due to creditors be settled within 30 days from receipt of an invoice unless a contract says otherwise. Section 38(1)(f) of the PFMA makes the accounting officer of each department responsible for it, and National Treasury Instruction 34 of 2011 forces departments to file monthly exception reports listing every invoice paid late or left unpaid, with reasons. Non-compliance can constitute financial misconduct for the officials responsible.

Knowing the mechanics matters more than knowing the principle. The 30 days runs from receipt of a valid invoice, so invoice correctly, get a dated acknowledgement of receipt, and reference your contract or order number exactly. If payment is late, escalate in writing to the department's chief financial officer citing Treasury Regulation 8.2.3, then to the relevant treasury. Late payment is a reportable failure with a paper trail, not a favour you must wait for politely.

## Where SMMEs realistically fit

Be honest about the ladder. Rung one is quotation work in your municipality: small, frequent, relationship-driven. Rung two is formal tenders sized for your capacity, where your bid must be complete, compliant and priced from real costs. Rung three is subcontracting on larger contracts, which the new regime actively encourages through subcontracting requirements on big awards. Skipping rungs is how small firms win contracts they cannot deliver, and non-delivery follows you through the system.

The system has real problems: late payments persist, and corruption risk is a topic we will cover on its own. But the architecture (CSD, eTenders, the 30-day rule, the coming set-asides) is public, free and usable. The SMMEs that win government work treat compliance as an asset they maintain weekly, not paperwork they scramble for the night before a deadline.
