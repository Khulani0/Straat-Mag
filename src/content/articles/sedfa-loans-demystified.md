Since the 2024 merger that folded SEFA, SEDA and the CBDA into the Small Enterprise Development and Finance Agency, SEDFA has been the front door for state loan finance to small business. The loan products built at SEFA continue under the new agency, and they remain the most accessible institutional debt in the country for a business that no bank will touch. Accessible does not mean casual: applications fail in predictable ways. Here is the product set and the failure modes.

## The products

Direct lending is the core: loans from R50,000 to R15 million made straight to SMMEs and co-operatives in any sector. The money can fund asset purchases, working capital, property, expansion, start-ups and acquisitions. Term loans dominate, alongside bridging finance built for a specific and very South African purpose: funding the execution of a signed contract or purchase order, where the money arrives to buy materials and pay wages, and repays when the client pays. For a business winning government work under the 30-day payment rule, bridging finance is the product that makes the contract deliverable.

Alongside direct lending sits wholesale finance: SEDFA lends to intermediaries (micro-finance institutions, co-operative financial institutions, retail finance intermediaries) that on-lend smaller amounts in places the agency cannot reach branch by branch. If your need is under R50,000, an intermediary funded through this channel is often where you actually end up, and that is by design.

Pricing follows SEDFA's risk-based model rather than a single advertised rate: fixed or floating, set per application. Development pricing generally undercuts what a commercial bank would charge an unsecured small business, but it is not free money, and it is not a grant. It must be repaid on schedule.

## What sinks applications

The published requirements read as an inverted list of the common rejections. The business must be registered and legally compliant: no CIPC registration, lapsed tax affairs or missing licences ends the conversation. It must operate primarily in South Africa. It must demonstrate viability: a plan whose numbers show the loan being repaid from real cash flow, not from optimism. And it must show sound management: financial records, even simple ones, that match the story the application tells.

Translate that into practice and the pattern is clear. Applications fail on incompleteness (missing documents, unsigned forms), on unviability (the numbers do not repay the loan), and on inconsistency (the bank statements contradict the business plan). None of these are about connections or luck. All of them are fixable before you apply, which is precisely why the fixable failures are so common: most applicants apply first and prepare afterwards.

## How to approach it

Treat a SEDFA application like a tender bid: a compliance pack plus a financial argument. The compliance pack is your registrations, tax status, licences and accounts, current and consistent. The financial argument is one page of honest arithmetic: what the loan buys, what that purchase earns, and the monthly repayment covered by that earning with room to spare. If you cannot write that page convincingly, the application is premature, and the honest move is to fix the business model, not to embellish the plan.

Two final notes. First, SEDFA's merger promise is that business support and finance now live in one institution: if your application is declined, ask what support programme addresses the gap, because that is now the same organisation's job. Second, the warning that applies to every programme in this magazine applies here too: applying is free, and anyone selling "guaranteed" SEDFA approvals is a fraud.
