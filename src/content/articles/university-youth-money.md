A registered South African student sits inside a funding ecosystem most founders would envy, and most students graduate without ever touching it. The channels below are all verified, all current, and all built specifically for people whose main asset is a student card.

## The InnoVarsity: the national stage

The EDHE Absa Entrepreneurship InnoVarsity (until this year the Entrepreneurship Intervarsity) is the national student competition across all 26 public universities, run by Universities South Africa's EDHE programme, backed in 2026 by a R5,5 million Absa partnership. Entries for 2026 opened on 16 April; students aged 18 to 35, undergraduate or postgraduate, any discipline, enter through their campus EDHE coordinator, and the 2026 edition carries up to R1,2 million in prize money plus bootcamp and mentorship. Over its first seven years the competition and its SAB Foundation partnership channelled R1,387,500 to 60 student startups. The InnoVarsity's real value compounds off-stage: campus winners get institutional attention (incubation space, faculty support, introductions) that no application form can buy.

## The SAB Foundation's university seed funds

Quieter than the awards, the SAB Foundation also runs university seed funds: started with UCT's Bertha Centre for Social Innovation and expanded to the University of Limpopo, University of Zululand and University of Venda, investing over R2,1 million in 27 student-linked ideas by March 2024, with the Limpopo fund alone distributing R400,000 through its student competition. If you study at one of these institutions, the seed fund is the most accessible first cheque on this page: designed for ideas, judged on social value, and administered by people whose job is developing you.

## TIA and the tech transfer office: for inventions

If your business is built on research (an engineering prototype, a lab result, an algorithm developed in your degree), the Technology Innovation Agency's Seed Fund flows through university technology transfer offices to move research outputs toward prototypes and commercialisation. Walk into your TTO before you graduate: funding eligibility and intellectual property rights both get more complicated after you leave, and the TTO's whole function is packaging student and staff inventions for exactly this money.

## The corporate competitions: tech's side door

Corporate developer and innovation challenges have become a genuine student funding channel. The freshest example: at Huawei's inaugural Code4Mzansi finals in May 2026, a student team's township commerce platform took the People's Choice Award and R100,000 (their story runs in this issue's Winners). These competitions reward working demonstrations over business plans, which suits students whose skills outrun their paperwork.

## And the standing infrastructure

Everything in our wider funding coverage applies to students too: the NYDA grant programme funds 18 to 35 year olds regardless of enrolment, and a student business that trades for a year becomes eligible for programmes like Tholoana. The strategic point for a student founder is sequencing: campus money first (seed funds, the InnoVarsity, corporate challenges), because it is judged developmentally and forgives inexperience; national money second, once there is trading history to show. Four years of subsidised infrastructure, faculty expertise and forgiving capital is a runway. Treat graduation as the deadline it is.
