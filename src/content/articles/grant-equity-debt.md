Every funding conversation eventually reduces to three instruments. A grant gives you money you never repay. Debt gives you money you repay with interest. Equity gives you money in exchange for a piece of the company forever. For a founder with no collateral, no rich family and no track record, the question is not which is best in theory. It is which will actually say yes, in what order, using instruments that exist in South Africa in 2026.

## Grants: cheapest money, smallest cheques, most patience

A grant costs you zero percent and zero shares. That makes it the best money in existence and everyone knows it, which is why grant programmes ration by process: eligibility rules, compulsory training, itemised quotes, monitoring.

The real instruments: the NYDA grant (18 to 35, 100 percent youth-owned, cumulative cap R200,000) and prize money from the competition circuit, which behaves like a grant with a pitch attached. Both fund equipment and stock, not salaries, and both take months.

The honest trade-off: grants buy your first machine, they do not build your company. Founders who chain grant applications as a business model spend more hours on forms than on customers. Take the free money once or twice, deliberately, for assets that raise your revenue, then move up the ladder.

## Debt: the only money that scales without diluting you, and the only one that can sink you

Debt keeps your company yours. Every rand of profit after repayments belongs to you, which is why established businesses prefer it. The catch for a zero-collateral founder used to be that no bank would look at you. Development finance changed that arithmetic: state and social lenders price for mission, not just security.

The real instruments: SEDFA (the former SEFA products) lends from roughly R50,000 to R15 million to registered SMMEs, with development mandates rather than bank-style collateral demands. The IDC's Gro-E Youth Scheme funds youth-majority businesses from R1 million at prime less 3 percent, concessionary pricing you will not find on any commercial term sheet. Old Mutual's Masisizane Fund lends R250,000 to R20 million to majority black-owned juristic entities, with a bias to women, youth and disability, and rural or township operations.

The honest trade-off: debt converts your future revenue into a fixed obligation. If your cash flow is a guess, a loan turns a bad quarter into a crisis. The discipline test: take debt only against revenue you can already see (a signed contract, a purchase order, a machine that demonstrably pays for itself), never against hope. And read the conditions: Masisizane, for instance, requires comprehensive insurance including SASRIA cover before it transfers a cent.

## Equity: expensive forever, but the only money that shares your risk

Sell 25 percent of your company and you have sold 25 percent of every rand of profit it ever makes. That is the most expensive capital there is, if you succeed. It is also the only capital that costs nothing if you fail, and the only kind that arrives with a partner whose incentives match yours.

The real instruments: for the "missing middle" (too big for micro-finance, too small for private equity), FNB's Vumela fund, managed with Edge Growth, makes equity investments and has built a venture debt product precisely so founders can scale without full dilution. The venture capital scene proper remains narrow and concentrated in tech.

The honest trade-off: equity is for businesses with a credible path to multiplying in size, because the investor needs an exit. A solid services company growing steadily is a debt business, not an equity business, and pitching it to investors wastes everyone's year.

## The sequence for a zero-collateral founder

Put together, the order writes itself. Start with grant-class money (NYDA, competitions) to acquire your first productive assets, because nobody else funds a business that does not exist yet. Use trading history, even six months of honest numbers, to unlock development debt sized to visible revenue. Reserve equity for the moment growth is constrained by capital alone and the business could genuinely be five times bigger. Skip a rung and the maths punishes you: debt without revenue defaults, equity without scale dilutes you for nothing. The founders who fund themselves well are rarely the ones with the best single deal. They are the ones who matched the instrument to the stage, every time.
