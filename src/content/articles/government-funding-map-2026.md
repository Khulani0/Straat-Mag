If your mental map of government funding still says SEFA for loans and SEDA for business support, your map is out of date. Both agencies were dissolved into a single body, and three new pots of money have appeared since. This is the verified state of play in July 2026: who funds what, at what ticket size, and where to apply.

## The big change: SEDFA replaced SEFA, SEDA and the CBDA

On 1 October 2024, under the National Small Enterprise Amendment Act of 2024, the Small Enterprise Finance Agency (SEFA), the Small Enterprise Development Agency (SEDA) and the Co-operative Banks Development Agency merged into one institution: the Small Enterprise Development and Finance Agency, SEDFA.

The practical effect is that finance and business support now sit behind one door. Where you previously applied to SEFA for a loan and separately to SEDA for training or incubation, SEDFA's mandate covers the full journey: pre-investment support, funding, and post-investment growth. The former SEFA loan products continue under SEDFA, spanning direct loans, bridging finance and asset finance, with reported ticket sizes running from roughly R50,000 to R15 million. Applications run through sedfa.org.za and SEDFA's branch network.

If a funder, consultant or website tells you to "go to SEDA", they are two years behind. That is a useful filter for who else to trust.

## The DSBD's money: TREP and the new Peo Pele Youth Fund

The Department of Small Business Development (DSBD) runs its flagship programmes through SEDFA as the implementing agency.

The Township and Rural Entrepreneurship Programme (TREP) is the one to know if your business operates in a township or rural area. It blends funding with business support for stock, equipment and working capital. Two things changed in the 2026/27 budget: the funding ceiling was raised from R1 million to R3 million per business, and R710 million was set aside for the programme. That is a materially bigger cheque than TREP could write last year.

The newest arrival is the Peo Pele Youth Fund, launched in QwaQwa during Youth Month 2026 by Small Business Development Minister Stella Tembisa Ndabeni. It is a R300 million fund for youth-owned businesses this year, inside a plan to invest roughly R900 million over three years, structured by business stage from start-up to growth. The terms are genuinely concessionary: finance at prime less 2 percent, a possible capital repayment holiday of up to 12 months, and training attached, because the fund explicitly builds on the lesson of the earlier Youth Challenge Fund that money alone is not enough. It is implemented through SEDFA, with around 750 youth-owned enterprises expected to benefit. If you are under 36 and own your business, this fund is new enough that the queue is still short.

## NYDA: the grant, not the loan

The National Youth Development Agency remains the main source of genuinely non-repayable money for young entrepreneurs. The grant programme covers South African citizens aged 18 to 35 with businesses that are 100 percent youth-owned, and tops out at a cumulative R200,000 per business (R250,000 for registered co-operatives and for agriculture or technology projects). Most first grants are far smaller: the tiers start at R1,000 to R10,000 for survivalist and idea-stage businesses. Turnover caps apply, and business management training is compulsory before money moves. We unpack the full criteria and process in a separate article this issue.

## IDC: when you need millions, not thousands

The Industrial Development Corporation plays in a different weight class. Its Gro-E Youth Scheme funds businesses that are more than 50 percent youth-owned (up to age 35) from R1 million to R50 million per project, at concessionary pricing of prime less 3 percent on loans. The catch is the mandate: the project must create jobs at a cost of no more than R500,000 per job, and the IDC expects real operational involvement from the youth shareholders, plus a path to BBBEE level 4 within 24 months. This is industrial-scale money for manufacturing, agro-processing and similar ventures, not survivalist funding.

## TIA: money for technology that does not exist yet

The Technology Innovation Agency fills the gap no bank will touch: turning a technology idea or research output into a prototype. Its Seed Fund supports innovators at universities and science councils (apply through your institution's technology transfer office) and technology SMMEs (through TIA and its regional partners). In 2026 TIA's seed fund and commercialisation hubs were allocated R137,3 million as part of a broader R1,2 billion boost to the agency.

## The map in one table

| Funder | Best for | Ticket size | Where |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SEDFA (former SEFA products) | Registered SMMEs and co-ops needing loans | About R50,000 to R15m | sedfa.org.za |
| TREP (DSBD via SEDFA) | Township and rural businesses | Up to R3m | sedfa.org.za |
| Peo Pele Youth Fund | Youth-owned businesses, all stages | R300m fund, stage-based | Via SEDFA |

| TIA Seed Fund | Tech prototypes and commercialisation | Project-based | tia.org.za, via TTOs |

One honest warning to close. Government money is slower than private money. Every programme above carries compliance requirements, and several carry compulsory training or support components. Build that time into your plan, and never pay anyone to "unlock" a government grant for you: applications to every programme on this map are free.
