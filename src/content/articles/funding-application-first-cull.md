Every funding programme in South Africa publishes its criteria, and every funding programme rejects most applicants for failing the published criteria. That is not a paradox, it is a filing problem: founders write applications about their dreams while screeners tick boxes about eligibility. The first cull (the pass through applications that eliminates the majority) is almost entirely mechanical, which means it is almost entirely avoidable. Here is how the real screens work, using the actual published rules.

## Screen one: hard eligibility. No appeal, no charm.

Every programme opens with binary gates. The NYDA grant requires citizens aged 18 to 35 with 100 percent youth-owned businesses under the turnover cap. Tholoana requires at least 51 percent black ownership, 12 months of trading, and a founder working in the business full time with no other employer. Africa's Business Heroes requires the applicant to be founder or co-founder, an African citizen or direct descendant, with a business at least three years old. The IDC's Gro-E Youth Scheme requires more than 50 percent youth shareholding and caps funding at R500,000 per job created. Masisizane lends only to juristic entities, majority black-owned, tax compliant.

Read your target's list before you write a word. If you fail a hard gate, applying is a waste of a week, and misrepresenting your way past one is fraud that surfaces at due diligence. The productive move when you fail a gate is sideways: another programme's gates differ.

## Screen two: completeness. The dumbest way to die.

The second cull removes incomplete applications, and it is ruthless because it is defensible: a funder distributing public or charitable money cannot exercise discretion for one applicant's missing tax pin. The NYDA process expects supplier quotes that match the requested items exactly, completed business management training, and a business bank account. Masisizane will not transfer approved funds without proof of comprehensive insurance including SASRIA. SEDFA expects registration, compliance and financial records.

The fix is clerical, not creative: build a compliance folder before application season (registration documents, tax status, bank confirmation, ID copies, licences, accounts) and update it monthly. The founders who submit complete applications on deadline day are not more diligent people; they simply did the boring work in the quiet weeks.

## Screen three: the fit between your ask and their mandate

Programmes exist to produce specific outcomes, and screeners score how directly your application produces them. The SAB Foundation's awards fund innovations benefiting women, youth, rural communities and persons with disabilities: an application that never mentions its beneficiaries fails regardless of how clever the product is. The IDC funds job creation at a capped cost per job: an application requesting R10 million to create four jobs is arithmetic self-elimination. ABH looks for established businesses with traction and African impact.

So write the funder's outcome into your numbers. State the jobs, the beneficiaries, the local content, whatever the mandate names, and make the figures honest and checkable. Screeners reward applications that do their scoring work for them.

## Screen four: numbers that survive contact

What remains after the mechanical culls is judged on viability, and the test is simpler than founders fear: do the numbers connect? Revenue claims should trace to customers or contracts, costs should look like the real world, and the funding requested should visibly convert into the growth projected. A modest, coherent plan beats a spectacular, incoherent one every time, because the assessor's own career depends on funded businesses surviving.

The meta-lesson is the cheapest edge in fundraising: the first cull tests preparation, not potential. Prepare like it matters, and you begin every application in the minority that gets read properly.
