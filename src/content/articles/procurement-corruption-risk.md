Corruption talk in South Africa is usually loud and vague. The Auditor-General's findings are quiet and specific, and they are the map an honest small business needs, because they show where the system actually breaks: not evenly, everywhere, but concentrated in particular processes and particular places.

## What the numbers say

The AG's latest national findings put irregular expenditure at R42,5 billion for the financial year, down from R49,5 billion the year before, with roughly R32 billion more still under review and excluded from the headline. Procurement and contract management are named as the main causes. Entities reporting irregularities of some kind accounted for 88 percent of government expenditure.

Local government is where the concentration turns severe. Across the outgoing administration's four years, metros and their entities racked up R73,87 billion in irregular expenditure, R23,14 billion of it in 2024-25 alone, and about 77 percent of that was procurement and contract non-compliance. Of 10,830 awards audited in 2024-25 (worth R65,12 billion), 85 percent of municipalities failed to comply with procurement legislation in making them. National Treasury separately flagged municipal irregular expenditure climbing to R268 billion cumulatively.

Read precisely: irregular expenditure is not all theft, it is spending that broke the rules, which spans everything from sloppy paperwork to engineered awards. But the AG's award-level findings, and case reporting like amaBhungane's work inside Eskom's R21 billion diesel contract, show the pattern honest bidders experience directly: specifications written for a chosen bidder, unsuited bidders securing tenders, and evaluation criteria applied unevenly.

## What this means for your bidding strategy

The risk is not uniform, so your strategy should not be either. Municipal procurement is, statistically, the roughest terrain: if you bid there, bid on work where the specification is objective (goods with clear standards, measurable services) rather than work where evaluation is subjective and manipulable. National departments and the better-run entities audit cleaner; the AG's reports, which are public, tell you which institutions get clean audits, and that list is effectively a customer-quality ranking no private-sector salesperson ever gets.

Then defend yourself with process. Keep every bid's records: the advert, your submission, the outcome letter. You are entitled to reasons for an award decision and to see the successful bid's disclosed details; ask for them in writing every time you lose, because a paper trail of unanswered questions is what regulators and journalists build cases from. If you see rigging (a specification only one company can meet, a briefing where the winner seems pre-known), you can object through the institution's dispute process, complain to the relevant treasury, or report to the Public Protector or the AG's material irregularity process. The Public Procurement Act regime now being finalised adds a dedicated tribunal architecture for exactly these disputes.

## The line you never cross

The other half of protection is internal. Never pay a "facilitation fee", never let an official's relative invoice you for "consulting", and never sign the SBD 4 disclosure or SBD 9 independent-bid certificate dishonestly: those forms are sworn statements, and they are what turns a corrupt request you went along with into your own fraud conviction. The honest-SMME playbook is unglamorous: bid where the rules hold, document everything, object formally, and walk away from any deal that requires your silence. The auditor's numbers say the system is compromised but watched. Position yourself on the side the watchers can defend.
