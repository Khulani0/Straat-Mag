Every awards evening produces the same photograph: the oversized cheque, the delighted founder, the sponsor's logo. The photograph never shows year two. This magazine profiles winners, so it owes readers the honest question: what does the evidence say happens after the prize, and what separates the winners who compound from the winners who stall?

## What the published evidence actually shows

The cleanest South African dataset comes from the SAB Foundation, which has published impact reporting across a decade of supporting entrepreneurs (more than R891 million invested to date). Its headline finding: around 90 percent of entrepreneurs it supported were still operating five years later, a figure the Foundation itself attributes not to the money alone but to the combination of financial and non-financial support sustained over time. Its reporting is explicit on the mechanism: consistent, practical support helps entrepreneurs navigate uncertainty and make better decisions, which is a careful way of saying that money without judgement leaks.

The prize designers have voted with their structures. Look at what South Africa's serious programmes actually award now: Pitch and Polish pays R650,000 cash but locks R350,000 into a two-year Raizcorp incubation bursary. Anzisha does not hand its fellows a lump sum and wave goodbye; it wraps them in a two-year venture-building fellowship with coaching and staged support. The SAB Foundation attaches mentorship and business development assistance to every award. When the institutions with the most winner data consistently convert cash into structured support, they are telling you what their post-prize experience taught them.

## Why cash-only winners stall

No published SA dataset tracks cash-only prize winners in isolation, so honesty requires reasoning from the structures rather than inventing a failure rate. But the stall pattern the programme designs guard against is visible in their rules: prize money arrives as a lump into a business whose systems were built for smaller flows. The classic failures follow: capacity bought before demand (equipment idle, staff hired ahead of revenue), the founder's calendar consumed by publicity instead of customers, and the win itself treated as validation of every assumption, ending the cheap experiments that got the business there. A prize is capital without a term sheet, and the absence of an investor's discipline is both its charm and its danger.

## The winner's playbook for year one

Spend the credibility before the cash. The award's real asset is that funders, corporates and suppliers now return your calls: convert that into contracts and accounts while the news is fresh, because reputational capital decays faster than money. Bank the money against a plan you already had, not a new dream the win inspired; if the plan was wrong last month, the cheque did not fix it. Take every hour of support the programme offers, because the evidence above says the support is the part that correlates with survival. And keep entering the calendar strategically: a win qualifies you for bigger rooms (this issue's calendar lists them), and serial credibility compounds.

The photograph is the start of the work. The winners who understand that are the ones we will still be writing about in five years.
