Ask a bank branch for a small business loan with no collateral and you will usually hear no. Ask the same bank's enterprise and supplier development (ESD) machinery and the answer changes, because that machinery exists to fund exactly the businesses the branch declines. Every big four bank runs one. None of them advertises it well. Here is the verified map.

## FNB: the Vumela fund

FNB's flagship is Vumela, managed with Edge Growth, an equity and venture debt fund for the "missing middle": businesses too big for micro-finance, too small for private equity, and short of collateral for bank credit. Vumela's first three phases deployed R404 million and created more than 6,500 jobs between 2010 and 2021; the current phase committed a further R200 million, with venture debt designed to fund growth without taking the founder's equity. Behind FNB sits new international firepower: the IFC extended a US$100 million loan to FirstRand in September 2025 specifically to expand MSME lending through FNB, with a separate risk-sharing facility covering half the credit risk on up to R1,8 billion of SME loans, targeted at women-owned businesses and climate-smart agriculture. The African Development Bank approved a further US$310 million package to FirstRand aimed at MSMEs, women entrepreneurs and agribusinesses.

## Absa: the SMME fund and the corporate pipes

Absa's ESD model works through its corporate clients: the corporate names the SMEs in its value chain it wants developed, and Absa administers the funding and support. For businesses holding government or corporate contracts, the Absa SMME Fund lends between R5,000 and R3 million over up to five years, targeting 100 percent black-owned businesses with awarded contracts, which makes it a contract-execution product in the same family as bridging finance. The scale of the corporate route showed in November 2025, when Absa and HEINEKEN Beverages launched a R1,2 billion drive for black-owned SMEs: R600 million in ESD funding from HEINEKEN administered by Absa, matched by R600 million in Absa co-lending. Absa also runs a procurement marketplace connecting SMMEs to buyers.

## Standard Bank: development programmes plus DFI scale

Standard Bank's ESD focuses on majority black-owned businesses, with enterprise development for emerging firms and supplier development for businesses in the bank's own supply chain. Its programme layer is unusually strong: a 12-week skills programme built to make businesses funding-ready, and the 12-month Basali Development Programme for women entrepreneurs. On the funding side, the African Development Bank and Standard Bank signed a package combining a R3,6 billion social bond investment with a US$200 million risk participation agreement, built to expand SMME lending and trade finance.

## Nedbank: the competition route and small business services

Nedbank's most visible SMME commitment is now Pitch and Polish, the national pitching competition it took over as headline backer (with Raizcorp), carrying a prize package worth over R1 million and a coaching programme that reaches far beyond the winners. Alongside it sits the bank's small business banking and the SimplyBiz network for business owners.

## How to actually use this map

Three practical readings. First, ESD money is B-BBEE-driven, so most of these programmes require majority black ownership; check the specific threshold before applying. Second, the fastest route in is often a contract, not an application: Absa's SMME Fund and much supplier development funding exists to finance work you have already won, so a purchase order in hand transforms your eligibility. Third, notice what the DFI deals mean: the IFC and AfDB are pushing hundreds of millions of dollars through FirstRand and Standard Bank with explicit targets for women-owned and agricultural businesses. That money only moves if qualifying businesses apply through the banks' normal channels, and loan officers now have institutional reasons to say yes to profiles they previously declined. If you fit a target category, say so in the application, explicitly.
