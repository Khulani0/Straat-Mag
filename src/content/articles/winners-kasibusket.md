The township economy runs on spaza shops, and spaza shops still run largely outside the digital economy: cash-based, invisible to suppliers' ordering systems, absent from the platforms where formal retail competes. KasiBusket, built by a student-led team, attacks exactly that gap, and at the finals of Huawei's inaugural Code4Mzansi developer competition in May 2026 it won the People's Choice Award and R100,000 in funding support.

## The team

The team is led by Tshegofatso Morena, a 20-year-old entrepreneur and chief executive of DevRift, alongside developer Kgodiso Lebese, a Computer Systems diploma student at Tshwane University of Technology, and Tebogo Seopa, pursuing a BSc Honours in Computer Engineering at Wits. That composition is worth noticing: a business lead and two builders, drawn from two institutions, competing against professional developers in a national competition and winning the audience's vote.

## The product

KasiBusket is a digital solution that helps spaza shops and small township businesses participate in the digital economy. The pitch that won the public over is the pitch that matters commercially: the township economy is enormous, loyal and underserved, and the shops that anchor it need tools built for their reality (data costs, cash flow rhythms, informal supply chains) rather than scaled-down versions of formal retail software. Code4Mzansi's finals showcased young developers building for South Africa's real economy, and KasiBusket was the crowd's answer to what that means.

## Why this win matters strategically

Three lessons travel beyond this team. First, corporate developer competitions have quietly become a funding channel for very young founders: Huawei's competition put R100,000 into a student-led venture on the strength of a working demonstration, no collateral, no trading history, no equity taken. Second, the People's Choice mechanism rewards something investors also price: the ability to make an audience care. A team that wins a public vote has already proven distribution instincts. Third, building for the township economy is now award-winning strategy rather than niche charity: from SUM1's stokvel financing (also profiled this issue) to KasiBusket's spaza tools, the money and the recognition are moving toward founders who treat kasi commerce as the mainstream market it is.

The R100,000 is seed fuel, not a finish line. But a 20-year-old chief executive with a funded product, a national award and two technical co-founders has assembled, before graduating age, exactly the package that the university funding channels, the NYDA tier and the youth competitions upstream of him are designed to accelerate. The pipeline works when founders work it.
