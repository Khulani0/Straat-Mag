The Ledger's founding premise is that the numbers tell you where the risk is, so this week's data piece takes government's own report card on the 30-day payment rule and reads it the way a supplier should: as a customer credit check.

## The dataset

National Treasury publishes an annual report on non-compliance with the 30-day payment requirement, compiled from the exception reports departments must file monthly. The 2023/24 edition, the most recent full-year picture, contains two headline numbers. At the end of March 2024, invoices older than 30 days and still unpaid stood at 114,908 invoices worth R10,7 billion. During the year, a further 362,068 invoices worth R35,1 billion were eventually paid, but paid late. Add the stock of unpaid to the flow of late and roughly R45,8 billion in supplier money spent time on the wrong side of the legal deadline in a single year.

## The chart that matters

The hero chart above shows the split that should drive your strategy. Of the R10,7 billion unpaid past deadline at year end, national departments accounted for R53 million across 1,427 invoices, an improvement of R13 million on the prior year. Provincial departments accounted for R10,6 billion across 113,481 invoices, a 53 percent regression in invoice numbers year on year.

Read that again as a ratio: provincial departments held roughly 200 times the late-payment value of national departments. The 30-day risk is not evenly distributed across "government". It is overwhelmingly a provincial phenomenon, and within provinces the exception reports historically concentrate in a handful of departments, health and education prominent among them, and in specific provinces. The Treasury report names names; before you take a large provincial contract, find your prospective client in it.

## What a supplier does with this

Three moves follow directly from the data. First, price the terms you will actually get: if your prospective client's paper shows a late-payment record, your quote needs a cash-flow buffer or your business needs bridging finance arranged before delivery, not after the crisis. Second, use the machinery that produced this dataset: departments must file monthly exception reports on invoices past 30 days, so a written escalation to the CFO citing Treasury Regulation 8.2.3, copied to the relevant treasury, lands on an existing compliance trail. Treasury's own report shows the escalation route works at the margin: it received 245 supplier queries worth R198 million in the year and resolved 54 of them worth R58 million into actual payment. Third, diversify deliberately: a supplier whose whole book is one provincial department is carrying concentrated credit risk that the data says is real.

The 30-day rule article earlier in this issue covers your rights. This one covers your pricing. The state is a customer whose payment behaviour is published annually, in detail, by its own treasury. Suppliers who read the report before signing have no excuse to be surprised, and suppliers who quote as if every sphere of government pays alike are subsidising the slow ones.
