The Ledger's founding premise is that the numbers tell you where the risk is, so this week's data piece takes government's own report card on the 30-day payment rule and reads it the way a supplier should: as a customer credit check.

## The dataset

National Treasury publishes an annual report on non-compliance with the 30-day payment requirement, compiled from the exception reports departments must file monthly. The 2024/25 edition, the latest, contains the headline that matters: at the end of March 2025, invoices older than 30 days and still unpaid stood at roughly R18,4 billion, and the trend is regressing. Provincial departments alone held 140,364 unpaid overdue invoices worth R18 billion, 24 percent more invoices than the 113,481 they held a year earlier, with the report noting a spike between August 2024 and March 2025. The flow of money paid late is similarly large: in 2023/24, the last full year of published paid-late data, 362,068 invoices worth R35,1 billion were eventually paid, but paid after the legal deadline.

## The chart that matters

The hero chart above shows the split that should drive your strategy. Of the unpaid pile at March 2025, eight national departments accounted for R381 million across 2,437 invoices. Provincial departments accounted for R18 billion across 140,364 invoices.

Read that as a ratio: provincial departments held roughly 47 times the overdue value of national departments. The 30-day risk is not evenly distributed across "government". It is overwhelmingly a provincial phenomenon, and within provinces the exception reports concentrate in a handful of departments, with health prominent: 70 percent of provincial-level supplier queries to Treasury related to health departments in the prior year's report. Treasury's reports name names; before you take a large provincial contract, find your prospective client in them.

## What a supplier does with this

Three moves follow directly from the data. First, price the terms you will actually get: if your prospective client's paper shows a late-payment record, your quote needs a cash-flow buffer or your business needs bridging finance arranged before delivery, not after the crisis. Second, use the machinery that produced this dataset: departments must file monthly exception reports on invoices past 30 days, so a written escalation to the CFO citing Treasury Regulation 8.2.3, copied to the relevant treasury, lands on an existing compliance trail. Treasury's own reporting shows the escalation route works at the margin: in 2023/24 it received 245 supplier queries worth R198 million and converted a portion into actual payment. Third, diversify deliberately: a supplier whose whole book is one provincial department is carrying concentrated credit risk that the data says is real and growing.

The 30-day rule article earlier in this issue covers your rights. This one covers your pricing. The state is a customer whose payment behaviour is published annually, in detail, by its own treasury. Suppliers who read the report before signing have no excuse to be surprised, and suppliers who quote as if every sphere of government pays alike are subsidising the slow ones.
