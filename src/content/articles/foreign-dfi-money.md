Founders regularly ask how to get World Bank or African Development Bank money, imagining an application form in Abidjan or Washington. The honest answer is that for a South African SMME, that form does not exist. Development finance institutions (DFIs) fund small businesses at wholesale: they lend to or share risk with local banks and funds, which then lend to you. Understanding the pipes tells you which local door the foreign money is behind.

## The bank pipes: FirstRand and Standard Bank

The largest recent flows run through the big banks. The IFC, the World Bank Group's private sector arm, extended a US$100 million loan to FirstRand in September 2025 to expand small business lending through FNB, alongside a risk-sharing facility that covers half the credit risk on up to R1,8 billion of SME loans, with women-owned businesses and climate-smart agriculture as priority targets. The African Development Bank approved a further US$310 million package for FirstRand aimed at MSMEs, women entrepreneurs and agribusinesses, and separately combined a R3,6 billion social bond investment with a US$200 million risk participation agreement at Standard Bank to expand SMME lending and trade finance.

What this means in practice: you do not apply to the IFC or the AfDB. You apply to FNB or Standard Bank through their ordinary business lending channels, and the DFI money changes the answer you get. Risk-sharing facilities exist precisely to make the bank approve profiles it used to decline: thinner collateral, women-owned, agricultural. If you fit a named target category, state it prominently in your application, because the bank has reporting obligations to its DFI partners and actively needs qualifying loans on its book.

## The fund pipes: Mastercard Foundation

The Mastercard Foundation runs two large vehicles relevant to this side of the continent. The Africa Growth Fund is a US$200 million fund-of-funds: it capitalises African investment funds, which in turn invest in early-stage, growth-oriented small businesses. The Fund for Resilience and Prosperity is a seven-year, US$126 million programme working through SMEs across 20 sub-Saharan countries to create dignified work for young people. Again, the route is indirect: the practical question is which investee funds and implementing partners operate in South Africa, and those funds' own application channels are the door. If you are raising equity at seed or growth stage, ask every fund you meet whether DFI or foundation capital sits behind them; those that carry it have explicit mandates (youth employment, gender) your business may satisfy.

## Reading the labels on the pipes

Every DFI deal is announced with target categories, and those categories are your eligibility signals. The current flows name three repeatedly: women-owned businesses, agriculture and agribusiness, and climate-adjacent activity. A woman running an agri-processing business in 2026 South Africa stands behind more institutional money than at any point in memory, but only if she applies through the banks and funds where the money actually sits.

Two cautions close the map. First, DFI-backed does not mean concessionary: the loans price commercially, the equity dilutes normally, and the discipline of the previous articles in this series still applies. Second, beware anyone selling access to "World Bank funding" for a fee. The pipes are public, the doors are the banks and funds named above, and walking through them costs nothing.
